# The Pigeon Beyond the Sky

*Before He Was Admiral Pigeon — Book Two*

*For Juliet*

## Chapter One: Pebble Through a Window

After several seasons in the Planetary Pigeon Airforce, Pigeon knew the shape of Pebble's weather. He knew which clouds carried rain sideways and which valleys collected mist. He had carried medicine over flooded orchards and messages through winds that made the ground appear to be traveling in the wrong direction.

Now the whole planet fitted inside a window.

Pigeon leaned toward the shuttle's glass. Somewhere in that blue-and-brown roundness was Old Wing Cavern, and somewhere in its rock wall was a little home containing three pigeons who had watched him leave.

Father Bram had packed enough seeds for a modest famine. Mother Tansy had repaired the tube holding the old family route cloth. Gran Cora had inspected the shuttle, the pilot, and a suspicious crumb under the departure bench.

“Keep your letters interesting,” she had told him. “Your father wants to hear about the stars. Your mother wants to hear that you're eating. I want to know what goes wrong.”

“Why?”

“That is usually where the story starts.”

Beside him, Bramble was trying to recover a seed that floated just beyond his beak.

“I understood that joining the Galactic Pigean Space Corps involved travel,” he said. “I hadn't expected my breakfast to join separately.”

Tiller caught the seed, bounced against the opposite wall, and drifted backward with a startled expression.

“Excellent catch,” Bramble said. “Let us know when you finish stopping.”

They had transferred together. Sergeant Flint had seen them off with three fierce coos, a warning about loose cargo, and a farewell so quick that Pigeon suspected the sergeant was feeling something inconvenient.

The shuttle curved toward a ship like a wide silver wing. Lamps glowed along its sides, and a row of transparent bubbles rested beneath an open shelter at the rear.

Gold letters on its hull read **HOMEWARD**.

“Good name,” Bramble murmured.

Inside, the floor held their feet again. Pigeon straightened his iridescent neck feathers as Captain Vela approached. She was a dark gray pigeon with a small notch in her beak and the unhurried walk of somebody who knew where every corridor went.

Their new perch plates waited in a neat row. Beneath each name were the words **SPACE CONVERSION FLIGHT**.

Tiller looked at his. “Conversion to what?”

“Pigeons who can stop,” said Vela.

Bramble made an appreciative sound.

The captain led them past a room full of charts. Through another window, Pebble dwindled behind the ship. Pigeon felt the uncomfortable sensation that his home might disappear if he blinked.

Vela stopped beside him.

“First time away from your world's sky?”

He nodded.

“The kitchens serve supper at the middle bell. Write home afterward. The mail launch leaves before the last.”

She continued down the corridor. Pigeon followed, grateful that she had given him something ordinary to do.

That evening he wrote that the stars were bright, he had eaten well, and Tiller had already lost a race against a seed.

He left room at the bottom of the page for whatever went wrong tomorrow.

## Chapter Two: The Problem with Stopping

The transparent flight bubble contained a perch, steering pedals, a little cargo cradle, and room for a pigeon to spread both wings. Beyond its shell lay space.

Pigeon looked for a breeze. His feathers remained perfectly still.

“Your bubble carries your air,” Vela said over the flight radio. “Its thrusters move you. Your wings and feet tell it where you want to go. The rest of space has very little interest in flapping.”

Pigeon pressed a pedal.

His bubble slid out of its cradle and into the training yard, a ring of floating marker hoops tethered to Homeward. The carrier filled half his view. Pebble filled rather less of it than he liked.

He banked toward the first hoop.

The bubble turned beautifully.

It continued traveling sideways.

Pigeon flapped harder. The bubble spun, and the hoop passed above him, below him, and above him again.

“I appear to have acquired several hoops,” he reported.

“Same hoop,” said Vela. “Ease your wings. Press the slowing pedal.”

He pressed it too sharply and bumped a padded marker. A tremendous warning bell sounded inside his bubble.

Tiller's bubble shot past. A moment later, another bell rang over the radio.

Bramble drifted through the first hoop at the speed of a thoughtful cupboard.

“You will be late,” Tiller told him.

“I will be upright.”

By the end of the lesson, Pigeon had traveled backward through two hoops, sideways through one, and around a fourth without entering it at all. He had once been a flyer whom younger birds watched from the training ledge. Now a machine was congratulating him for arriving approximately near his own perch.

He stayed after the others landed.

Vela waited beside the launch rail.

“Could I have another turn?”

“You can. What will you change?”

Pigeon opened his beak. Practice harder sounded useful until he considered that he had already been trying extremely hard.

“I keep expecting the air to catch me,” he admitted.

“Then begin with a smaller journey.”

She moved two markers close together. Pigeon traveled from one to the other, stopped, and came back. Nothing rang. He tried again. Soon he could feel the little thrusters answering the pedals instead of fighting the missing wind.

When he returned, Bramble was waiting with a bowl of supper.

“Tiller says he meant to turn upside down.”

“Did he?”

“All seven times, apparently.”

Pigeon ate beside the window. Far away, sunlight touched Pebble's edge. He wanted to hear the familiar echo of Old Wing Cavern, and the wanting surprised him with its strength.

He took out the route cloth. Its faded stitches showed a thousand small decisions made by pigeons who had flown before him: shelter here, wait there, take the lower passage when the rain comes.

On the cloth's plain reverse, he sketched two hoops.

Underneath, he wrote: **I can stop between these now.**

It seemed too small for a letter home. He copied the drawing onto his letter anyway and sent the letter. The cloth went back into its tube beside his perch.

## Chapter Three: An Extremely Serious Deck

The next morning, Pigeon found Dr. Nib kneeling beside a bowl of something that no breakfast should contain.

“Don't eat that,” said Nib.

“I wasn't going to.”

“Excellent. We have avoided my least enjoyable explanation.”

Nib was a small pigeon whose laboratory occupied a corner of the upper rear flight deck. It contained neatly labeled drawers, untidily opened drawers, diagrams, tools, and one drawer labeled **THINGS WHICH ESCAPED THE OTHER DRAWERS**.

Above the door hung a polished sign: **POOP DECK**.

Pigeon looked from the sign to the bowl.

“Is that why—”

“The name came with ships long before I arrived,” Nib said. “We pigeons have simply made certain adjustments to its meaning.”

The deck crew collected droppings during the daily cleaning. Most went to the carrier's gardens. Some came to Nib, who was investigating whether pigeon poop could become the most embarrassing equipment in military history.

He pointed to a small sealed pod.

“Poop and stardew gel. Entirely experimental. Do stand behind the screen.”

The pod struck a practice lens with a splendid splat.

A pale, lumpy smear spread across the glass.

From the viewing booth, Tiller said, “The universe has become porridge.”

“You see?” Nib cried. “Ordinary droppings wouldn't spread properly out there. The gel helps them cling to an exterior lens.”

“For how long?” asked Bramble.

Nib pressed a control. A shutter closed over the lens. Something scrubbed behind it. When it opened, a clean strip revealed Tiller's eye.

“That,” Nib said, with less enthusiasm, “remains a discouraging question.”

Captain Vela arrived as the second shutter cleared the rest.

“An obscured observation lens,” she said. “Still a working ship. Still instruments and radio. Still an annoyed crew.”

“An extremely annoyed crew,” Nib agreed. “Science should be precise.”

She approved bubble trials against empty targets.

Pigeon carried one pod beneath his capsule. He approached the practice lens, aimed, and released.

The pod sailed past it.

From his new angle he watched it pass a marker hoop, miss the deck rail, and splat across the laboratory's outside window.

There was a long silence.

“A useful discovery,” Nib announced over the radio. “My window was in the wrong place.”

They spent the afternoon learning how a released pod kept traveling with its bubble's motion. Bramble was best at judging the load. Tiller was best at reaching awkward angles. Pigeon improved when he stopped thinking of throwing anything and began thinking of where everything was already going.

At supper, Nib appeared carrying an enormous diagram. A brown streak decorated one corner.

“The targeting problem is solvable,” he said.

“And your window?” asked Pigeon.

Nib considered this.

“My view has become more private.”

## Chapter Four: The Long Way Round

Their first formation exercise was a cargo run through the practice booms. Vela put Pigeon in charge.

The assignment warmed him all the way to his feet. At last there was something familiar: a route, a flock, a load to deliver.

Bramble's bubble carried a wide medical crate. Tiller would scout. Pigeon would lead.

“My left cradle is sticking,” Bramble said during departure checks.

“Will it hold?” Pigeon asked.

“Yes. It changes how closely I can turn around the—”

“We can use the middle line. I know that section.”

Vela's voice came over the radio. “Flight ready?”

Pigeon answered before anyone else could.

They entered the booms smoothly. Tiller called the first opening clear. Pigeon rounded a tight corner, felt the familiar satisfaction of a good turn, and looked back.

Bramble's crate had caught beneath a padded crossbar.

His bubble hung awkwardly to one side. Each movement pressed the crate harder against the boom.

“Stop,” Vela said.

Everything stopped except Pigeon's stomach, which seemed to fall a considerable distance.

A deck tug came to release the load. The exercise clock continued running. Tiller drifted beside Pigeon without speaking, which felt worse than anything he could have said.

Back aboard, Pigeon began explaining the turn.

Bramble interrupted him.

“I knew about the turn. I was telling you about the crate.”

Pigeon shut his beak.

Vela set the route diagram between them.

“Show me the flight you planned.”

Pigeon traced it with one claw.

“Now show me the flight Bramble could make.”

That route went around the outer boom. It was longer. Pigeon had dismissed it without asking.

The captain left the diagram with them.

For a while the only sound was the tug returning to its rail.

“I'm sorry,” Pigeon said. “You were giving me something I needed. I decided I already had it.”

Bramble examined the diagram.

“I would like the long way round.”

“Then we'll take it.”

They repeated the exercise the next morning. Before departure, Pigeon asked each flyer to describe the thing most likely to cause trouble. Tiller needed a wider stopping space after his scouting dash. Bramble needed room for the cradle. Pigeon admitted he might become impatient.

“I'll put that on the cargo list,” Bramble said. “Dangerous goods.”

They finished without a tug.

A letter from home waited on Pigeon's perch. Tansy had praised his little drawing of the hoops. Bram reported that the rooftop peas had defeated a neighboring gardener's fence. Gran Cora had added a line beneath them both.

**I like knowing where you are while you learn. Send the next map.**

Pigeon drew the long way round. This time he included three bubbles.

## Chapter Five: Three Ships Waiting

The distress call arrived during a lesson about docking clamps.

One moment Tiller was arguing that a clamp should look more obviously unclamped. The next, every lamp along the deck changed to amber.

Vela called the crew to the chart room.

Three little lights blinked beside a broken moon.

“The Hearth Convoy,” she said. “Apricot, Kettle, and Lantern. Food, water, medicine, and families returning to Mallow Station.”

Pigeon knew Mallow from the cargo notices. It was a garden station where enormous glass houses caught the sunlight, and where somebody apparently ordered watering cans by the hundred.

“Why have they stopped?”

Vela enlarged the chart.

The moon lay inside a thick belt of rock fragments. The broad channel through them was Larkspur Passage. Across its exit waited four dark ships.

“Iron Talon,” she said. “They arrived after the convoy entered. Commander Grint has closed the passage.”

Tiller's neck feathers lifted. “He can't close somebody else's passage.”

“He has,” Vela replied.

A recorded message sounded from the chart table.

“By authority of Marshal Clang, all cargo is to be surrendered for inspection and reassignment. Compliance will ensure orderly travel.”

The voice was crisp and comfortable. Pigeon disliked it more because it sounded as though it were announcing the opening of a seed shop.

The convoy's return route was much longer. The freighters had already spent fuel reaching the passage. They could wait in the sheltered loading basin or surrender their cargo. They could not take their passengers on a grand voyage around the broken moon.

“Those supplies belong to Mallow,” Bramble said.

“Yes.”

Homeward would approach the passage exit while the convoy remained sheltered. Vela had requested allied help; the nearest patrol ships were still too far away to reach them before the freighters would have to make their choice.

As the others discussed Grint's ships, Pigeon studied the moon.

A thin dotted line disappeared beneath the main channel. Half its label was hidden by a newer notice.

“What's that?” he asked.

Nib shifted the notice. **OLD LAMP SERVICE ROUTE. SMALL VESSELS ONLY.**

“Maintenance launches used it to reach the channel lights,” Vela said. “The lamp towers were retired when the navigation buoys arrived.”

“Does it still go through?”

“The chart can't tell us.”

Pigeon leaned closer. The line followed the sheltered side of the larger rocks. It reminded him of the ledges on the route cloth, where a pigeon could stop when the main air passage was too dangerous.

“Could we look?”

Vela considered the route, then the three waiting ships.

“We can look. Two scouts, two separate accounts. A dotted line is somebody's old information.”

Tiller stepped beside Pigeon.

“I'll take the first account.”

“And I'll take the slower one,” Pigeon said.

From the chart table came a new message. The captain of Lantern was asking whether Mallow's children would still get their medicine.

Vela answered immediately.

“We are here. Keep your ships together. We are working on your route.”

## Chapter Six: The Places Between

From a window, the broken moon had looked like a cracked bowl surrounded by crumbs.

Inside its ring, the crumbs were mountains.

Pigeon and Tiller followed a navigation buoy into shadow. Their bubbles lit the rough surface of a rock that turned so slowly Pigeon first mistook it for something still.

“It moves,” Tiller said.

“I see.”

“You see now. I saw first.”

Pigeon smiled despite the hollow feeling beneath his feathers.

They found the first retired lamp tower lying along a cliff of stone. A service rail continued beneath it. Tiller followed the rail while Pigeon recorded the openings and the rocks around them.

They traveled slowly. Even Tiller traveled slowly.

Their radios clicked with brief observations: narrow here, loose fragments there, clear space beyond. Homeward received each report from its position outside the ring. The little service route was becoming a present-day map.

At the second tower, Pigeon saw an opening beyond two overlapping ridges.

“Straight through?”

Tiller hovered beside it.

“Wait.”

The near ridge drifted across the far one. The inviting gap closed.

Pigeon felt his claws tighten on the perch.

“I thought that was one rock.”

“Two,” Tiller said. “I thought so too until the shadows moved.”

They waited for it to open again, then watched a second time. There was room for a bubble easily, and perhaps for a freighter if it entered at the right moment. Beyond lay a deep pocket sheltered on three sides.

Pigeon marked a waiting place.

Near the far end, an old ore-hauling berth widened the route. A faded sign showed a barge much larger than either scout's bubble.

“The maintenance launches shared a road,” Pigeon said.

“And somebody forgot to give it a new label.”

Tiller went ahead to inspect the last bend.

An Iron Talon patrol boat appeared across the opening.

Pigeon pressed his slowing pedal. His bubble stopped beneath the old berth's lip. Tiller tucked behind a broken lamp housing, scarcely farther away than a wingbeat.

The patrol boat's white observation lens turned toward the rocks.

Pigeon could hear his own breathing. He wanted to ask whether Tiller was safe. Their radio silence had been agreed before departure. He kept his beak closed.

The lens paused.

Then the boat passed on, its thrusters stirring pale dust from the rock face.

Tiller emerged after it disappeared.

“I dislike being interesting to that boat.”

“Same.”

At the exit, two great shelves of stone made a second moving opening. They watched a complete turn before measuring the widest gap against the freighters' charted shapes. Apricot and Lantern could make the turns. Kettle's side loads were too wide.

Back aboard Homeward, Bramble studied their drawings.

“We could bring the loads inboard,” he said. “Move the water tanks along the center rack.”

“While she's waiting in the basin?” Vela asked.

“If her captain lets me touch his cargo arrangement.”

The captain of Kettle joined them by radio. He heard Bramble out, objected to three details, and accepted a fourth arrangement they devised together.

Pigeon added it to the map.

The dotted line had acquired corners, warnings, and somewhere to wait.

## Chapter Seven: Cargo and Courage

Homeward could not enter the service route. Captain Vela positioned her behind the moon's broad shadow and sent the three pigeons to the convoy by the sheltered approach.

The freighters were old and practical. Apricot had mismatched loading doors. Kettle carried water tanks that made her resemble a kettle somebody had sat on. Lantern's clinic windows glowed warmly above her cargo hold.

Captain Hobb of Kettle met Bramble at the docking rail.

“Nothing has fallen off this ship in eleven seasons.”

“Encouraging,” said Bramble. “We'll try to keep the run going.”

While they reorganized the tanks, Pigeon visited Lantern.

A young fieldmouse passenger stood beside the window with a folded paper bird. She watched his bubble being brought inside.

“Does yours fly by itself?” she asked, pointing to the capsule.

“It has thrusters,” Pigeon told her. “I tell them where to go.”

She considered this and looked down at the paper bird.

“Mine has me.”

The captain brought Pigeon to a chart screen. He explained the narrow route and the waiting pocket. She asked about the turns. He showed her Tiller's observations. She asked about the patrol boat. He told her that too.

“And if we have to stop?”

“You stop. We won't ask you to guess what the ship ahead is doing.”

The captain studied him for a moment, then began assigning her crew to their stations.

Back at the dock, Nib was talking through a display beside the splat-pod rack.

“Remember, you carry a small number of embarrassing interruptions. They remain interruptions, even when spectacular.”

Pigeon described the patrol boat's polished front lens.

“My favorite kind,” Nib said. “Observe the shutters as well.”

Their plan was straightforward enough to frighten Pigeon. Homeward would show herself at the main exit and demand passage. As Grint's ships turned toward the carrier, the pigeons would smear the patrol boat's observation and aiming lenses. The convoy would begin entering the service route during the confusion.

They would continue only while Tiller's scouting reports showed the way clear.

Vela spoke to each captain, then each pigeon. Bramble had finished moving the water tanks and verified Kettle's turns with Hobb. Tiller knew which rocks could hide his bubble. Pigeon knew where the three freighters must wait.

“Captain,” he said privately, “what if the confusion isn't long enough?”

“Then you bring them into shelter and tell me. You have permission to stop this plan.”

He had wanted command because it sounded like being the bird who said go. The word stop felt heavier.

Before returning to his bubble, he saw the young passenger again. She had placed her paper bird on Lantern's window ledge, pointing toward Mallow.

Pigeon took three steps along the docking rail, stopped, and looked back at his friends.

Bramble closed the last cargo catch. Tiller settled onto his perch.

They were ready.

## Chapter Eight: A Very Brief Victory

Homeward emerged into sunlight.

“Commander Grint,” Vela called. “These civilian ships are traveling to Mallow Station. Open the passage.”

“Inspection is compulsory,” Grint replied.

“Taking their food is not an inspection.”

The Iron Talon ships turned. Their polished lenses flashed white.

Beside the service route, Tiller rose over the patrol boat's viewing turret. Pigeon approached below it. Each released a pod.

Two splendid splats blossomed across the front glass.

On the open radio, somebody shouted, “What is on the window?”

Somebody else shouted, “Don't wipe it sideways!”

The patrol boat swerved away from a rock. Pigeon felt a wild burst of joy. After all the missed targets, the stained laboratory window, and the patient adjustments, the ridiculous thing had worked.

“Apricot, begin,” he called.

The first freighter slipped beneath the retired lamp tower. Kettle followed with her tanks tucked close. Lantern waited until the route behind Kettle was clear.

Then metal shutters snapped across the patrol boat's face.

“Front view lost,” its pilot reported. “Using flank observation.”

A second lens opened farther aft. The boat corrected its course. A thin amber clamp-beam reached toward Apricot, searching for a steady grip on her hull.

“Pigeon, another eye,” Tiller called.

He released his remaining pod at the flank lens. The smear caught its edge. The beam slipped sideways and struck empty rock.

“Convoy hold,” Pigeon said. “Apricot, into the marked pocket. Kettle, follow. Lantern, remain behind the first tower.”

Captain Hobb was already turning. A loose cargo strap drifted into Kettle's side thruster guard and caught.

“Restricted turn,” he reported.

Bramble moved alongside in his bubble. Its cargo arm hooked the strap and pulled it free. Kettle slid into the pocket behind Apricot as the patrol boat regained its view.

Pigeon waited above the turn until Lantern had backed into shelter. The amber beam swept past him, close enough to fill his bubble with yellow light.

He pressed the pedal and dropped beneath a stone ledge.

No one spoke for several breaths.

Then Bramble said, “Everyone is here.”

Pigeon's claws gripped the perch so tightly they ached.

Grint's voice crossed the radio.

“Auxiliary patrol, watch the side channels. The carrier is a distraction. No cargo is to leave this sector.”

Their small attack had revealed that the convoy was trying to move. Two patrol boats now watched the ring instead of one.

Pigeon reported every part of it to Vela, including how little time the splats had bought.

“Remain in shelter,” she said. “We'll work from where you are.”

He stared at the route map. Three waiting ships. Two watchers. Far fewer pods.

Through Lantern's distant window he could just see a tiny white shape.

The paper bird still pointed toward Mallow.

## Chapter Nine: A Map with Everyone on It

Pigeon returned to Lantern's dock to speak with her captain. The fieldmouse child was asleep beneath the window. Her paper bird had tipped onto one wing.

He wanted, suddenly and fiercely, to be in the little home in the wall. He wanted Bram complaining about stubborn seeds, Tansy searching for a tool beneath the shelf, Gran Cora pretending she had never been worried about anything.

Instead he opened the channel to the other ships.

“Tell me what each of you needs.”

Apricot needed time to turn before accelerating. Kettle needed the outside curve because of her length. Lantern had used precious fuel reversing into shelter; she needed a steady journey with as few stops as possible.

Pigeon redrew their approach.

Tiller returned from another careful look. “The second patrol boat waits beyond the final bend. It can't see through the moving ridge. I can lead it toward the opening on the other side.”

“Can you get away?”

“Through the lamp arch. A bubble fits. The boat won't.”

“If it stays where it is?”

“I'll tell you before the ships leave the pocket.”

Bramble laid out their remaining pods. Three.

Nib listened from Homeward, making an occasional scratching sound as he drew.

“We used them too early,” Pigeon said. “Before, we hoped the boat would stay confused while the convoy traveled. Now we need the convoy near the last turn first.”

“And both viewing faces obscured together,” Nib added. “The instruments still show that a ship is present. Those little aiming eyes give its clamp-beam the close view needed to hold a moving hull.”

“How do we keep the convoy hidden until then?” Lantern's captain asked.

Pigeon showed her the sequence of sheltered pockets, including one Tiller had just found near the exit. Apricot and Kettle would move first. Lantern would follow while they waited out of sight. Then they would cross the last opening together, keeping the clinic ship's journey steady.

Vela examined the plan.

“Homeward will enter the broad passage and occupy the main ships. When you emerge, come beneath our wing. We'll escort you the rest of the way.”

Bramble passed one of his pods to Pigeon. He kept the other.

“One for each face,” he said. “One spare.”

Their bubble gauges showed ample breathing air and less generous thruster reserves. They had been maneuvering for a long time.

“Mine has the strongest towing arm,” Bramble said. “I'll remain at the rear until everyone clears.”

Pigeon drew Bramble's position on the map, then Tiller's escape through the arch, then Homeward waiting beyond the rocks.

The page was crowded. He could find every friend on it.

Lantern's captain studied the final turn and nodded.

Pigeon returned to his bubble. Before the shell closed, he looked once more toward the window.

The child had woken. She set her paper bird upright and gave it a small, determined push toward the glass.

## Chapter Ten: The Last Lamp

Tiller went first.

Pigeon watched his friend's bubble glide along the underside of the broken lamp tower. It appeared for a moment against the stars, then vanished beyond the ridge.

The radio remained quiet.

Apricot's captain asked whether to move.

“Wait for Tiller,” Pigeon said.

Far beyond them, Homeward entered the main channel. The Iron Talon ships shifted to block her. Vela held her course until their clamp-beams brightened, then curved across their bows, making them turn with her.

“Your convoy cannot leave,” Grint announced.

“It has somewhere to be,” Vela replied.

Tiller's voice returned.

“Second boat following. I'm at the arch.”

Something struck metal with a distant clang transmitted through his bubble radio.

“Tiller?”

“Loose sign. It says DO NOT OBSTRUCT. Sensible advice.”

His bubble slipped through. The larger patrol boat slowed outside, searching for a way around the tower.

“Route clear,” Tiller said. “Beginning my return.”

Apricot left the waiting pocket. Kettle followed along the outer curve, turning exactly as Bramble and Hobb had planned. When they reached the final shelter, Lantern began her steady passage between the rocks.

Pigeon traveled beside the clinic ship. He could see the paper bird in the window and the child pointing him out to somebody taller.

At the overlapping ridges, Lantern's captain eased her ship forward.

The gap narrowed.

Pigeon felt the old urge to hurry everyone.

Tiller called from ahead. “Opening again. Hold your course.”

The ridges parted. Lantern slid through, close enough for lamplight to reveal pebbles in the stone.

All three ships reached the last shelter.

Pigeon and Bramble took their positions on opposite sides of the watching patrol boat. Beneath its front shutter, the observation lens gleamed clean. Its flank aiming eye searched the ring.

“Ready,” Bramble said.

“Homeward ready,” Vela answered.

Pigeon counted the convoy captains' replies. Apricot. Kettle. Lantern.

He released his first pod.

Bramble released his.

The splats landed almost together, one broad and magnificent, the other shaped like a rude question mark.

“Cross now,” Pigeon called.

Apricot emerged into open space. Kettle followed. The patrol boat's shutters slammed closed, and its clamp-beam groped through the freighters' wake without finding a grip.

Homeward curved toward them, shields glowing as Grint's ships tried to follow her turn.

For a moment, Pigeon could see the whole route working: Tiller watching the rocks, Bramble guarding the rear, the freighters moving, the carrier waiting.

Then the second patrol boat rounded the lamp tower.

Its lens was clean.

Lantern had nearly cleared the rocks when an amber beam fastened to her stern.

The clinic ship jerked backward.

“Caught,” her captain said.

Behind her, the two great shelves at the exit continued their slow, indifferent movement toward each other.

## Chapter Eleven: Bring Me Along

Pigeon turned toward Lantern.

The patrol boat held her just outside the gap. Her engines glowed as she struggled against its clamp-beam. Within her windows, small figures hurried away from the tilting floor.

“Ease your engines,” Bramble told her captain. “Save enough to leave when we free you.”

He brought his bubble toward the clinic ship. Its cargo arm caught her rear towing loop. His thrusters could help steady the stern, although they could never pull a freighter free of a patrol boat.

Pigeon had one pod.

The enemy's front lens faced Lantern. A long shutter hood protected it from above. Pigeon could approach underneath, close to the amber beam, and release from there.

His reserve light glowed.

He could make the approach. He could not make it and return to Homeward alone.

“Bramble,” he said. “If I use the last of my return fuel, can you collect me?”

Bramble looked across the gap between them.

“Yes. Show me where you'll be.”

Pigeon marked a point beyond the patrol boat's lower rail. Once he released the pod, his bubble's motion would carry him there. He would have enough thrust for one slowing correction.

“Tiller, watch my path.”

“Watching.”

Pigeon pressed the pedal.

His bubble moved toward the amber beam. Lantern's broad hull rose on one side of him. On the other waited the patrol boat, large enough to make his little capsule feel like a seed.

“Unidentified bubble, turn away,” its pilot ordered.

Pigeon kept going.

The boat's lower rail approached. He could see scrapes on the metal and a small dent beneath the lens. Behind the glass, a crew member leaned forward, suddenly understanding what the pod carried.

The shutter began to close.

Pigeon released.

The pod reached the glass just ahead of the shutter and spread across it in a glorious, appalling splat.

The clamp-beam wavered.

“Lantern, now!”

The captain fired her engines. Bramble steadied the stern as the ship pulled away, then released her towing loop.

Lantern slid clear.

Behind her, the shelves closed with a slow scattering of stones.

Pigeon made his last correction. His bubble slowed beside the lower rail, then drifted with the small remaining motion he had been unable to cancel.

The steering pedals answered with a hollow warning tone.

“I'm here,” he said.

The patrol boat was already scrubbing its lens.

Pigeon could do nothing useful with his wings. He folded them, listened to the air moving inside his shell, and watched the turning stars.

“Loose fragment on your left,” Tiller called.

Bramble's bubble appeared above him. Its cargo arm reached out, missed his docking ring, and withdrew.

The fragment passed between them.

For one dreadful moment, Bramble disappeared behind it.

Then the arm returned. A clamp closed around Pigeon's ring with the most beautiful click he had ever heard.

“Got you,” Bramble said.

He pulled Pigeon beneath the old lamp arch. Tiller led them through its narrow opening as the patrol boat regained its view. The boat turned to pursue and found a stone ridge where its route should have been.

Beyond the ring, Homeward's broad wing sheltered three freighters.

Vela's deck crew brought the bubbles in. When Pigeon's shell opened, his feet refused to leave the perch.

Bramble waited beside him.

At last Pigeon climbed out and leaned against his friend's sturdy shoulder.

“I was frightened,” he said.

“I noticed,” Bramble replied. “You sounded very polite.”

Pigeon laughed. The laugh shook, then settled.

From the flight-deck window he watched Lantern take her place behind Kettle. Three ships together, traveling toward their destination.

## Chapter Twelve: The Name on the Perch

Mallow Station welcomed the convoy with open docking arms and an argument about where to put so many watering cans.

The gardeners wanted the cans delivered first. The cooks wanted the food. The clinic wanted its medicine, which settled the matter immediately.

Pigeon watched Lantern unload beneath a canopy of bright leaves. Families embraced. Cargo trolleys squeaked. A tired captain sat down halfway through a sentence and fell asleep before reaching the end.

The fieldmouse child found Pigeon near the docking rail.

She offered him the paper bird.

“It arrived,” she said.

Pigeon took it gently in his beak. One wing was crooked. He liked that wing best.

Homeward stayed while the freighters refueled and the station counted its supplies. Allied patrol ships arrived to escort later traffic. The Iron Talon blockade at Larkspur still existed; for now, travelers would use another route under escort. Vela sent the scouts' complete chart to the ships responsible for the region.

Then she called Pigeon to the poop deck.

The crew had gathered beneath the launch shelter. Nib had cleaned his laboratory window for the occasion, although a stubborn pale crescent remained in one corner.

Vela stood beside a new perch plate.

“The corps has confirmed your appointment,” she told Pigeon. “Flight Lieutenant. Command of Homeward's new passage patrol.”

Pigeon stared at the words beneath his name.

“Bramble and Tiller have agreed to join you,” Vela continued. “More flyers will arrive after conversion training. Your work will be scouting, escort, and rescue along the routes that need you.”

“And experiments,” Nib added.

“Reviewed experiments,” said Vela.

Nib nodded solemnly. Behind him, a drawer opened by itself and deposited three diagrams on the floor.

Vela described what the whole crew had done: the route Tiller had found, the cargo Bramble had moved, the captains who had guided their ships, the engineering, the carrier's maneuver. When she spoke of Pigeon's final flight, he looked toward the friend who had towed him home.

Bramble inclined his head.

Afterward, Pigeon wrote a long letter.

He told Tansy about the meals, Bram about Mallow's gardens, and Gran Cora exactly what had gone wrong. He explained the rank and drew the route, including the waiting places. He described the sound of Bramble's towing clamp.

He left out none of the fear.

Their reply caught Homeward at the next mail station. Bram enclosed a drawing of a watering can far too large for the rooftop garden. Tansy asked whether the new perch was comfortable. Gran Cora wanted to meet Dr. Nib.

**I have questions,** she wrote. **Your mother says I should put them in a separate letter.**

Pigeon placed the reply beside the route cloth and the paper bird.

Beyond his cabin, new orders arrived. Marshal Clang had seized another travel gate. More ships needed escorts. The map of the Bellflower Galaxy was filling with warnings.

There would be other flights, other mistakes, and much more to learn before anybody called him Admiral.

For now, three bubbles waited on the deck.

Pigeon walked toward them, took three steps, stopped, and looked back.

His patrol followed.
