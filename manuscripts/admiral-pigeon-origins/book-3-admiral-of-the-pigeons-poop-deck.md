# Admiral of the Pigeon's Poop Deck

*Before He Was Admiral Pigeon — Book Three*

*For Juliet*

## Chapter One: A Crumb on the Commander's Map

The most important map on Commander Pigeon's desk had a crumb on it.

It was a very ordinary crumb. Father Bram had accidentally folded it into a letter from home, and Pigeon had accidentally eaten half of it before noticing that the letter said, *That is a piece of your mother's new seed loaf. Tell her it survived the journey.*

The remaining half rested on the blue circle marking Pebble.

Beyond that circle stretched the Bellflower Galaxy: curling star roads, scattered worlds, travel gates, and a growing number of red marks.

Pigeon studied the red marks.

Years had passed since his first patrol command. He had escorted harvest ships through meteor weather, searched for a lost school ferry, and once negotiated the release of a survey crew from a moon whose entire government was a deeply offended tortoise. He had earned promotions, made mistakes, learned names, and discovered that commanding several flights required considerably more listening than commanding one.

He was still a small gray pigeon.

His neck still shone turquoise and violet when he turned toward a lamp. He still wore neither clothes nor hat. The handsome plate beside his command perch carried his rank, leaving Pigeon himself free to look exactly like someone who might steal your sandwich.

There was a knock at the doorway.

“Commander,” cooed Bramble, “the emergency meeting has become more emergency.”

Pigeon covered the letter with the old family route cloth, freshly unrolled from the tube Mother Tansy had repaired. Its faded lines showed Old Wing Cavern's shelter ledges and storm passages. His own learning maps covered patches of the reverse. He kept it nearby because a map drawn by someone who expected you to come home was pleasant company.

On Homeward's command deck, Captain Vela stood before a bright image of Bellflower Gate.

The enormous silver ring connected their part of the galaxy to the refuge worlds beyond. Through its center shimmered a road of folded starlight. Along that road waited hospital ships, crowded passenger vessels, and freighters carrying families among sacks of grain.

Outside the ring, dark ships were gathering.

“Marshal Clang,” said Vela.

His Iron Talon Fleet had begun with blockades at distant passages. Now it occupied gates between whole groups of worlds. Ships carrying medicine paid his tolls. Ships carrying objections were turned back. The struggle to reopen those routes had become the Great War of the Bellflower Galaxy.

Bellflower Gate was the last open crossing for the evacuation.

“If he takes the control station,” Vela said, “he can close the road with the convoy on this side.”

Tiller leaned toward the image. “And use everyone waiting there to make the other worlds surrender.”

Vela nodded.

Pigeon examined the positions of the enemy ships, the gate, and the scattered allied escorts.

“How many pigeon flights?” he asked.

“Yours, plus the three arriving from the western stations.”

“Rescue tugs?”

“Four.”

“Food aboard the civilian ships?”

Vela's expression changed. “Some have enough for two days.”

Pigeon straightened.

“Then we'd better begin before breakfast.”

He returned to his cabin long enough to put the letter safely away. He ate the last crumb, which had survived its journey magnificently, and smoothed one folded corner of the route cloth.

Then he went to meet the flock.

## Chapter Two: The Gate and the Teacup

Bellflower Gate was so large that Homeward's shadow disappeared against one of its silver ribs.

The ring enclosed a soft blue shimmer. Beyond it, stars from the refuge worlds shone in the wrong places, as though someone had opened a window into another sky.

Pigeon had crossed gates before. He still liked that part.

He liked the ships waiting beside this one considerably less.

They crowded the approach in a long, ragged line. Blankets hung behind cabin windows. A little shuttle had a garden tied down in its luggage rack. One cargo vessel carried a hand-painted sign saying PLEASE DO NOT BUMP. BABY ASLEEP.

“Which baby?” asked Bramble over the radio.

“Could be anybody,” Tiller answered.

“Excellent sign, then.”

Their flight bubbles drifted above Homeward's upper rear deck. Inside each transparent capsule, a pigeon could stretch both wings, breathe comfortably, and steer with perch pedals and small movements. The bubbles' thrusters did the work outside; flapping alone would have achieved very little in space, however determined the pigeon.

Ahead, Tiller's scouts watched the Iron Talon Fleet.

Clang's flagship was called the *Crown of Order*. It resembled an enormous black teapot that had quarreled with the universe and grown cannons.

Around it waited five cruisers. Their polished observation lenses glinted.

Below the gate's lowest rib hung the Bellhouse, a control station shaped like a little silver bell. Allied workers still held it. Enemy landing boats were already moving closer.

“They won't try to destroy the gate,” Vela said over the command channel. “Clang wants the crossing. Our job is to keep it open and keep those people moving.”

Pigeon divided his flights. Tiller would scout the cruisers. Bramble would work beside the rescue tugs. Two flights would escort the civilians. The remaining pigeons would carry splat pods.

Dr. Nib arrived last, riding a maintenance platform loaded with canisters.

“Please remember,” he announced, “these are precision instruments.”

Bramble looked at a canister.

“They contain poop.”

“Precisely.”

Nib's stardew gel helped the pigeon droppings spread across the outside of viewing lenses even in space. The mixture could spoil an enemy's aim and produce a deeply satisfying expression on the other side of the glass. It could not stop engines or switch off instruments.

“We need a short opening,” said Pigeon. “Three transports are ready to cross. We cover the nearest cruiser's aiming eyes while their escorts move.”

A civilian captain joined the channel. Captain Marr was a fieldmouse with a tired voice and an astonishingly large ship called the *Teacup*.

“My passengers are strapped in,” she said. “The kitchen has become a nursery. The nursery has become a second nursery. We're ready.”

Pigeon looked toward the nearest cruiser.

“Tiller?”

“Lenses exposed. No landing boats in your line.”

“Bramble?”

“Rescue team ready.”

Pigeon glanced from bubble to bubble.

Then he gave the order.

“Let's make a terrible impression.”

## Chapter Three: A Very Clean Problem

The first splat struck the cruiser directly across its largest observation eye.

The second arrived beside it.

The third made the shape of a dancing turnip.

“That one was mine,” Bramble remarked from the rescue channel.

“You're not on the attack flight,” Pigeon said.

“No. I packed it.”

White streaks spread over black glass. The cruiser's nearest aiming turret hesitated, searching with its instruments while its view disappeared. Allied escorts moved into the opening. The first transport slid toward the blue shimmer, followed by a hospital ship.

For a moment, the plan worked beautifully.

Then the cruiser blinked.

A metal shutter swept down over the smeared eye. Behind it came a hum, a spray of violet cleaning foam, and the rapid squeak of an enormous mechanical wiper.

The shutter lifted.

The lens was spotless.

“Oh,” said Nib.

Smaller shutters clicked over the aiming eyes. The cruiser turned toward the bubbles, following them on its instruments.

“Break away,” Pigeon ordered.

The attack flight scattered. Bright warning pulses flashed past. A pulse clipped a bubble's outer guard, sending its occupant tumbling toward the gate.

Pigeon swung after it.

“Flight Three, keep your escort position. Bramble, take the damaged bubble.”

“Already moving.”

Bramble caught the bubble with a rescue line and steadied it before it reached the silver rib.

The hospital ship crossed.

The Teacup did not.

One of the freighter's old engines shuddered and stopped. Its nose drifted sideways. Captain Marr called for help while the ships behind her braked in a confusion of flashing lamps.

Two rescue tugs hurried forward.

At the far side of the ring, enemy landing boats swept toward the Bellhouse. An allied escort turned to stop them, leaving a gap in the defense of the convoy. Vela moved Homeward into that gap, placing the carrier's broad hull between the crowded ships and the nearest cruiser.

“We can't repeat the run,” Tiller reported. “Every ship has cleaning shutters.”

Pigeon watched violet foam vanish from a second lens.

They had allowed for cleaning shutters. They had met slower ones at Larkspur Passage, years earlier. These new machines had cleared the glass before the third transport could even begin its crossing.

“Attack flights, return with your remaining pods,” he said. “No second pass.”

A young flyer asked whether they might simply use more.

“Then we'd have a bigger mess and the same clean windows,” Pigeon replied. “Come home.”

On the Bellhouse, an orange lamp began flashing.

An enemy landing boat had reached the station.

Vela's voice came through, tight and clear. “The workers are withdrawing into the lower rooms. Clang's crew has the main controls.”

Along the gate, the first great closing rib moved.

The road of starlight narrowed.

Pigeon looked at the waiting convoy, the enemy cruisers, and the little bubbles coming back to Homeward.

The first attack had saved two ships.

It had also shown him exactly how the next one would fail.

## Chapter Four: The Wrong People to Rescue

The Teacup's passengers could see Homeward through their windows.

They could also see flashes blooming against its armor.

Captain Marr spoke so calmly over the radio that Pigeon suspected she was making a tremendous effort.

“The tugs have us. We can move if they get a clear pull.”

“Bramble is clearing the lane,” he told her.

Near the freighter's stern, Bramble's formation guided a broken cargo frame away from the towing lines. A basket of stuffed animals had escaped from the frame. They floated past in solemn procession: a bear, a rabbit, a squid in pajamas.

Bramble caught the squid in his bubble's cargo basket.

“Useful for morale,” he said.

Then an Iron Talon landing boat spun into view.

An allied escort had struck its steering section while defending the Bellhouse. Its engine flickered. One wing scraped the cargo frame, and the boat began turning helplessly toward the Teacup's exposed side.

“Enemy boat,” a rescue flyer called. “Shall we push it clear?”

Pigeon saw two shapes behind its window.

He also saw the tug nearest it begin to pull away, keeping its passengers beyond danger.

“Catch it,” he said.

The rescue channel went quiet for one breath.

“We have people waiting,” a tug captain said.

“I know. Bramble, keep the Teacup moving. I'll take the boat. One line from the rear tug, please.”

Pigeon's bubble darted behind the spinning craft. He carried the line around its damaged wing while the tug paid out slack. His first approach failed. The boat turned too sharply, and the line slipped across smooth metal.

He went around again.

In the window, a moth pressed both forelegs against the glass.

Pigeon could not hear him. He could see his mouth moving.

The broken wing swung toward the bubble.

Pigeon dropped beneath it, felt his capsule ring like a tapped bowl, and hooked the line around the wing's root.

“Pull gently,” he said.

The line tightened.

The boat's wild turning slowed. The tug drew it away from the Teacup, and Captain Marr's ship began moving again, back into shelter behind Homeward until the crossing lane cleared.

Pigeon lifted a trembling wing from the line-release control.

On his display, Tiller's signal flashed.

“The Crown's moving. I had a clear approach to its rear observation deck. It's gone now.”

“I saw.”

Pigeon had spent the opening on a rescue. Clang's ship settled behind its cruisers, harder to reach than before.

“Come back,” he told Tiller. “We need a different opening.”

The rescued boat reached Homeward carrying a moth called Lieutenant Rusk and a very young beetle pilot who kept saying he had never landed without permission before.

Rusk stared at Pigeon when the medical crew opened their hatch.

“We were trying to take your station,” he said.

“Yes,” Pigeon answered.

“You knew that?”

“Yes.”

Bramble arrived with the pajama squid tucked under one wing.

“Medical room's this way,” he told them. “The squid is spoken for.”

## Chapter Five: What the Map Left Out

Pigeon spread the gate charts across his cabin floor.

The command desk was too small. The charts were too many. His rank had failed to make either problem disappear.

Tiller brought observations. Nib brought old station plans. Vela brought the news that Clang had stopped the closing ribs halfway.

“He wants the convoy to see that he can finish,” she said. “He's demanding our surrender.”

The Bellhouse workers had escaped through their lower loading hatch in a little service shuttle. They were aboard Homeward now, furious about the loss of their station and eager to help recover it.

“How long?”

“Until the next station bell.”

Beyond the cabin window, the narrowing crossing shone like a tired eye.

Pigeon moved a paper cup off an old freight chart and found a looping row of dots beneath it.

“What's this?”

“The former cargo circuit,” Nib said. “Freight sleds used to go around the gate while their loads were sorted. Closed when the big transport docks were built.”

“Can a ship use it?”

“A ship? No. Too many old supports.”

“A bubble?”

Nib leaned closer.

“Possibly.”

Pigeon touched the faded family cloth beside the charts. Gran Cora's route marks showed something the splendid military map did not: little dots for shelter, a cross for a place to turn around, paired marks where flyers should wait for one another.

He had been staring at lines of attack.

He began marking places where small flyers could stop, watch, and choose their next move.

The freight circuit would never carry the convoy. It might let pigeon flights spread around the battle before Clang realized where they had gone.

Rusk came to the doorway with a medical attendant.

“Your captain said I could tell you something,” he said.

Pigeon stepped away from the charts.

Rusk's antennae drooped. “Our landing boats are supposed to wait for the Crown's permission before changing course. Even if the course is wrong. Our steering was broken, and I kept asking which direction we were allowed to fall.”

“What did they say?”

“Stand by.”

No one spoke for a moment.

“The cruisers do it too,” Rusk continued. “Clang wants every important turn approved from his observation deck. He says one mind makes a perfect fleet.”

Tiller's feathers lifted.

“One mind had better be watching an awful lot of windows.”

“His ship still has instruments,” Pigeon said. “So do the cruisers. And they'll keep using them.”

“Exactly,” Vela replied slowly. “They'll know where we are. The question is how quickly they'll decide what to do.”

Pigeon looked down at the small waiting places he had drawn around the ring.

“Tiller, scout the freight circuit. Mark what is actually there. Nib, find out what those old signal lamps can do.”

“And me?” asked Bramble.

“Tell me how many rescues your formation can manage without asking me first.”

Bramble considered him.

“All the ones that need doing.”

Pigeon nodded.

“Good. That's going in the plan.”

## Chapter Six: The Practice Nobody Clapped For

The old freight circuit looked like a necklace somebody had left in a shed.

Bent supports held empty loading platforms. Faded number plates drifted on loose hinges. An enormous painted arrow pointed toward a dock that had not existed for years.

Tiller passed it in his bubble.

“Very helpful,” he told the arrow.

His scouts divided, each taking a short part of the loop. They checked clearances, found places to shelter, and reported directly to the neighboring team as well as to Homeward.

One passage on Nib's chart had collapsed. Another was filled with old cable. Tiller crossed both off.

Behind the sixth loading platform, he found a clear view of the Crown's observation deck.

“We can reach the outside of the flagship's rear eye from here,” he reported. “Only small groups. Anyone following the old chart without looking will have a very short afternoon.”

Nib, meanwhile, had persuaded a row of ancient freight lamps to glow.

He stood on a maintenance cradle, surrounded by wires belonging to the cradle, looking delighted with everything except one purple lamp.

“That one's been purple since it left the factory,” he said. “It has no intention of changing.”

The lamps could signal across the gate even if radio chatter became confused. Nib fitted the same simple signal controls to the flight leaders' bubbles: wait, move, help.

Three signals. Visible ones.

“Could we have a signal for magnificent victory?” Tiller asked when he returned.

“Yes,” said Nib. “Afterward, wave.”

The plan took shape across the floor of Homeward's empty cargo bay.

Vela's escorts would pressure one side of Clang's formation. The pigeons would spread along the freight circuit, then cover several sets of observation lenses at different moments. Cleaners would still work. Instruments would still show the allied ships moving.

What Clang would lose was the comfortable instant when all his windows agreed and everyone waited for his instruction.

During those short delays, paired pigeon flights would guide allied ships into their next positions. Bramble's rescuers would clear damaged craft. A station recovery ship would approach the Bellhouse with the workers who knew its controls.

Every team had a task and a neighboring team to help.

Pigeon did not give them one grand movement to memorize.

He asked what they would do when it went wrong.

Vela moved a model cruiser into the recovery ship's path.

“This one turns early.”

“My escorts hold it here,” said a flight leader, moving her pebble.

Nib covered a lamp.

“That signal fails.”

“I use the next station,” said Tiller. “And tell my neighbor.”

Bramble tipped over Pigeon's model bubble.

Everyone looked at him.

“It happens,” Bramble said.

Pigeon looked at the little bubble lying on its side.

Then he asked Vela to name his replacement for every decision he had been keeping to himself.

They practiced until the plan no longer depended on a single pigeon remaining in exactly the right place.

No one applauded.

Outside, the station bell began its warning chime.

## Chapter Seven: All the Small Wings

The poop deck smelled of warm machinery, seed biscuits, and history being made in a bucket.

Nib inspected the last splat pods while the flights prepared to launch.

Around Pigeon, bubbles rose into their cradles. Some held flyers he had known for years. Others held young pigeons who had only recently learned that space did not become less enormous after your first trip into it.

He wanted to say something splendid.

He wanted, briefly, to sound like the brave warriors in Gran Cora's stories.

Then he noticed a young flyer trying to adjust her perch with both feet at once.

“One foot,” he told her. “Then the other.”

She settled into place and gave an embarrassed coo.

“I did that during training,” he said.

“Really?”

“I also went backward.”

Her feathers loosened.

Pigeon turned toward the assembled flights.

“You know your work. You know who is beside you. If something changes, use what you see and tell the others. We need everyone looking.”

He took three steps toward his bubble, stopped, and looked back.

Across the deck, the last flyer settled into her cradle.

Then they launched.

The small capsules slipped beneath the gate's silver ribs. Tiller's scouts led them around broken platforms, past hanging cables, and into waiting places along the old freight circuit.

Homeward moved forward with the allied escorts.

Clang's voice rolled across every open channel.

“Your time is concluded. Submit your ships to inspection and your worlds to my protection.”

Captain Marr whispered, “I'd rather submit a complaint.”

Vela answered the marshal.

“The civilian crossing will remain open.”

The Crown's forward turrets turned.

At the Bellhouse, the orange lamps brightened. The great ribs began closing again.

“First flights,” Pigeon said. “Now.”

Pigeons burst from the platforms in small groups. Splat pods arced toward three cruisers, striking observation eyes and the outer lenses of aiming towers.

Shutters clapped down.

Violet foam hissed.

While those ships cleaned their windows, Vela's left escorts changed position. Before Clang could approve an answering turn, another flight smeared the Crown's side observation deck.

Its instruments showed the escorts moving. Its officers called for new positions. Through the glass, they saw something that looked like a fried egg having a difficult day.

“Hold formation,” Clang ordered. “I will assess.”

By the time his shutters opened, Homeward had moved too.

“Second flights,” Pigeon said.

At the far end of the ring, Nib's first freight lamp shone.

The station recovery ship began its approach to the Bellhouse. Its pigeon guides flew ahead, marking a path between two allied escorts.

On the Teacup, Captain Marr brought her surviving engine to life.

One transport crossed the shimmering road.

Then another.

The entire gate seemed to be moving at once: cruisers, escorts, rescue tugs, bubbles, shutters, lamps, and little ships carrying gardens.

Pigeon checked his map.

Every flight was where it needed to be.

Then the cruiser on the right turned without waiting for Clang.

## Chapter Eight: The Commander Who Wasn't There

The cruiser came around hard.

Its captain had understood the attack. She kept her shutters down, trusted her instruments, and aimed for the old freight circuit.

“Scatter from Platform Six,” Tiller called.

A warning pulse struck its outer rail. The platform folded against a support. Two freight lamps vanished behind flying scraps of metal.

Pigeons darted toward the next shelter.

Tiller stayed long enough to check the last scout had cleared the rail.

The rail hit his bubble.

Pigeon saw the capsule spin into the gap between a platform and the gate's outer rim.

“Tiller, report.”

“Air holding. Thrusters jammed. I'm lodged in a place that appears to have been designed for a smaller disaster.”

Pigeon looked at his display. Bramble's rescue formation was towing a civilian shuttle. The nearest spare tug was beyond the gate. Pigeon himself was three platforms away.

Vela called. “The right cruiser is threatening the Bellhouse approach. Shall I bring Homeward across?”

Doing so would expose the carrier's damaged side.

Leaving the cruiser alone would expose the recovery ship, whose passengers were the only people nearby who knew how to undo Clang's gate commands.

Pigeon could hear Homeward's alarms through Vela's open channel.

“Can your armor take the crossing?” he asked.

“One crossing. I won't promise two.”

“Make it. Take control of the flight timing while I recover Tiller.”

“Understood.”

He sent his neighboring leader the changed plan and turned toward the damaged platform.

Behind him, the attack continued.

That felt strange.

He had given orders from the middle of things for so long that some small, foolish part of him expected the whole battle to pause when he left his place.

It did not.

Homeward swung across the cruiser. Allied escorts closed behind it. Two pigeon flights changed their targets together, covering the cruiser's side eyes as its captain tried to follow the recovery ship.

Pigeon reached Tiller.

The scout's bubble was caught beneath a twisted bracket. Its thrusters glowed uselessly against the metal.

“Turn them off,” Pigeon said. “You're pushing the wrong part.”

“I had noticed the absence of progress.”

Pigeon fastened his rescue line to Tiller's capsule. He pulled. The bracket held.

He stopped before the line broke.

The gap widened at one end. Tiller needed to roll sideways, then slide out along the curve.

“Left pedal. Fold your right wing. Slowly.”

Together, they rotated the trapped bubble.

Metal scraped across its guard. The bracket slipped free.

At that moment, another pulse struck the platform.

Pigeon's bubble jolted. His radio burst into a loud crackle and went silent.

The two capsules drifted into open space, tied together.

Beyond them, Homeward's damaged side flashed under another blow.

Pigeon pressed his radio pedal.

Nothing.

He had no voice on the command channel.

Then a freight lamp winked from the next platform.

Wait.

A second lamp answered farther around the ring.

Move.

His flock was still speaking.

## Chapter Nine: A Sky Full of Decisions

Pigeon tapped his help signal.

His lamp flashed through the bubble's transparent wall.

Across the gap, a scout answered. A rescue bubble peeled away from the next platform, collected Tiller's line, and began towing him toward shelter.

Tiller pressed a wing against his window.

Pigeon did the same.

Then he turned toward the Bellhouse.

Without his radio, he could no longer direct distant flights. He could still see the recovery ship approaching the station. He could see a damaged freight lamp hanging dark above its docking lane.

He moved into that lamp's old position and made his bubble light show the lane was clear.

The recovery pilot saw him.

The ship slid past.

Inside it, gate workers waited beside an allied boarding crew. Their leader carried a battered toolbox tied shut with string. She had spent half her life keeping the Bellhouse working. Clang had given her eight minutes to leave it.

She intended to have it back.

The recovery ship docked beneath the station. Its crew entered through the workers' loading hatch while allied escorts held the enemy landing boats away.

At the Crown, the marshal's observation shutters lifted.

A fresh splat landed on the glass.

The shutters closed again.

On a cruiser to his left, the view cleared just as two allied ships crossed its bow. Its captain requested permission to turn. Another captain reported a rescue tug in the firing lane. A third announced that the gate station had been boarded.

The instruments were working perfectly.

They showed Clang a battle changing faster than he could command it.

“Hold your positions!” he thundered.

Several captains did.

The cruiser that had turned independently tried to withdraw, only to find Vela's escorts across its path. Its captain lowered her guns and asked for terms.

Homeward answered at once.

Around the gate, allied ships moved through the openings the pigeons had made. One took position behind the Crown. Two more approached from the cleared side. The remaining Iron Talon cruisers had to turn away from the civilian lane to protect their flagship.

Bramble used that opening immediately.

His rescuers pulled the stalled shuttle toward the crossing. Captain Marr followed with the Teacup, towing the little ship whose garden was tied to its roof.

Pigeon watched the sign PLEASE DO NOT BUMP disappear into folded starlight.

Inside the Bellhouse, the gate workers reached the closing controls.

The station's orange warning lamp went out.

For a dreadful moment, nothing else happened.

Then the nearest silver rib began moving outward.

The blue road widened.

The Bellhouse's great bell rang once, clear across the radio channels Pigeon could no longer hear.

He saw its signal lamps blaze green.

The gate was open.

The Crown turned toward it.

Clang was trying to escape through the same crossing he had threatened to close.

Pigeon watched three pigeon flights spread above the flagship. Their leaders checked one another's positions. Two covered the outer observation eyes while the third waited for the next cleaning cycle.

The marshal's view vanished under a magnificent, uneven curtain of white.

He still had his instruments.

They showed Homeward directly ahead, allied escorts behind, and rescue ships carrying civilians across the only open route.

There was no clear passage for his flagship.

Vela's demand arrived on the Crown's bridge.

“Stop your engines. Lower your weapons. Your crews will be cared for.”

Clang ordered a new turn. The navigator showed him the waiting allied ships.

Clang demanded a clean window. The window was cleaned.

The allied ships were still there.

At last, the Crown's weapons folded against its hull.

One cruiser followed, then another.

Pigeon saw the surrender lights rise around Bellflower Gate.

He puffed his chest so suddenly that he bumped the inside of his bubble.

## Chapter Ten: The End of the Great War

The celebration began with a very serious complaint.

“You,” Nib told Pigeon, “have broken your radio.”

Pigeon stood on Homeward's poop deck while engineers opened his battered capsule.

“I noticed.”

“You have also bent a perfectly good landing guard.”

“I noticed that too.”

Nib inspected the damage, then stepped forward and pressed his forehead against Pigeon's.

“Excellent,” he said softly. “Keep noticing things.”

Tiller sat nearby with a bruised wing supported by the medical perch. Bramble placed the pajama squid beside him.

“Loan,” Bramble said. “Its owner is aboard the Teacup.”

Vela arrived with news from the Bellhouse. The workers were safe. The gate was steady. Captain Marr had delivered her passengers to the refuge side and requested permission to come back for more.

“After she fixes that engine,” Vela added.

Pigeon looked past her at the Crown, held between allied escorts. Its enormous windows glittered beneath the patient work of cleaning machines.

“Is it over?” a young flyer asked.

“This battle is,” Vela answered. “Now we make sure the other gates hear what happened.”

Clang's surrender included orders to his remaining forces to cease the blockades and release the occupied crossings. Allied representatives carried those orders with the news that the Crown and its guarding fleet had been captured intact.

Some stations opened at once.

Others needed days of talks. At two gates, local commanders refused until their crews demanded food, medical care, and a way home. Allied fleets kept the crossings watched while envoys settled the terms.

The work took weeks.

Homeward escorted supplies through roads that had been closed for months. Bramble organized the return of damaged ships. Tiller's scouts checked reopened routes. Pigeon spent three entire afternoons deciding which stranded passengers should board which transports, and considered this more exhausting than being shot at.

Rusk helped locate Iron Talon crews whose ships had lost contact with their commanders. He stayed aboard a medical transport until the last of those crews had been collected.

Letters arrived from worlds Pigeon had never visited.

One contained a drawing of a pigeon larger than a planet.

Another contained a thank-you from Captain Marr, together with the news that the pajama squid had returned to its owner and received a promotion to Bed Admiral.

At last, the final occupied crossing reopened under the agreed peace terms.

The Great War of the Bellflower Galaxy had ended.

There were ships to mend, families to find, and arguments that would take much longer than a war to settle. There were also markets reopening, schools calling children home, and seed freighters arriving with nobody demanding a toll at cannon-point.

For the first time in a long while, Pigeon removed the red marks from his map.

He left the names of the worlds.

He added the places where his flights had waited for one another.

Then a message arrived requesting his presence at an official ceremony.

Bramble read it over his shoulder.

“Dress uniform,” he said.

Pigeon looked at him.

Bramble's beak twitched.

“Fortunately, you're already wearing it.”

## Chapter Eleven: An Extremely Important Poop Deck

The ceremony took place above Bellflower Gate.

The reopened crossing shone beneath a broad viewing hall. Beyond its windows, civilian ships passed in both directions. Pigeon liked that better than any decoration the organizers had put inside.

They had put rather a lot of decoration inside.

Long ribbons looped over the audience perches. Flags marked the allied worlds. A polished command plate stood beneath a cloth on the central platform.

Mother Tansy sat beside Father Bram and Gran Cora.

Sergeant Flint had come from the Planetary Pigeon Airforce. He looked exactly as Pigeon remembered, including the expression that suggested somebody's landing could still use work.

Tiller's wing was healing. Nib had brought a notebook in case the decorations gave him an idea. Bramble had found the refreshments.

Vela stepped onto the platform.

She spoke about the gate workers, civilian captains, rescuers, escort crews, engineers, and scouts. She named the flights that had held their places and the ones that had changed their plans when the battle changed around them.

Then she called Pigeon forward.

He walked up the little ramp.

From there, the hall looked enormous.

He could see Mother Tansy's proud eyes, Father's chest puffed nearly round, and Gran Cora leaning forward as if she intended to remember every word accurately enough to correct the newspapers.

“On behalf of the Corps Council,” Vela said, “for courageous command at Bellflower Gate, and for the years of service, judgment, and care that made that command possible, you are promoted to the rank of Admiral.”

She uncovered the plate.

“Your appointment is Admiral of the Galactic Pigean Space Corps Poop Deck.”

The carrier flocks would now look to him to guide their service, from their first launches to their greatest adventures.

There was a small silence.

Somewhere in the audience, a child giggled.

Then another did.

Pigeon looked at the shining words.

ADMIRAL OF THE GALACTIC PIGEAN SPACE CORPS POOP DECK.

He had imagined medals when he was small. He had imagined flags, daring rescues, and perhaps an impressive amount of marching.

He had never imagined quite so much poop.

He puffed his chest.

The applause rolled through the hall like weather in a great cave.

When it quieted, Pigeon stepped toward the speaking perch.

“I would like it recorded,” he said, “that the poop was a team effort.”

The laughter was even louder than the applause.

Afterward, there were greetings and congratulations, three photographers who could never catch his neck shining at the same time, and a ceremonial seed cake that Bramble regarded as a logistical problem worth solving.

Sergeant Flint approached last.

“Admiral,” he said.

“Sergeant.”

“Did you count your flock?”

“Before we left. While we were out. When we came home.”

Flint nodded once.

Then he smiled.

Pigeon discovered that the smile mattered quite a lot.

Two days later, with his new plate carefully packed for delivery aboard Homeward, he flew back to Pebble with his family.

There was something he wanted to see before beginning his first assignment as admiral.

It was considerably smaller than a galaxy.

## Chapter Twelve: The Little Home in the Wall

Old Wing Cavern smelled of cool stone and supper.

Light from the rooftop gardens fell through the high openings. Wings rustled on the ledges. Somewhere deep in the cavern, a pigeon was arguing with an echo and losing.

Pigeon landed outside the little home in the wall.

The doorway had not grown.

Neither had he, very much.

Inside stood the same bowls, the same crooked shelf, and Father's gardening tools leaning where Mother Tansy said they were never meant to lean.

Gran Cora settled on her usual perch.

“Well,” she said, “are admirals still allowed to eat crumbs?”

“It's encouraged,” Pigeon answered.

Mother Tansy brought out a seed loaf.

Father Bram examined it with great attention.

“Your last one survived the journey,” Pigeon told her.

“All of it?”

“Until I found it.”

They ate together while the cavern darkened beyond the window.

Pigeon unrolled the route cloth on the floor. Gran Cora traced Messenger Fern's old path with one claw. On the reverse, Pigeon showed her a new drawing of Bellflower Gate, with little marks for the places where his flights had waited.

“These ones are Tiller's observations,” he said. “That clear line belongs to Bramble. Nib brought these lamps back.”

Gran Cora examined the drawing for a long time.

“You'll have to tell me the whole thing.”

So he did.

He told her about the cleaning shutters, Captain Marr's enormous Teacup, the ruined platform, and the moment he had lost his radio and found his flock carrying on.

When he reached the surrender, Father Bram let out a breath he seemed to have been keeping for several weeks.

They stayed up until the ordinary bowls were empty and the mighty Admiral of the Poop Deck had become sleepy enough to lean against his mother.

The next morning, a letter awaited him.

It bore the mark of Starling Academy, on the moon Luma in the Prism Galaxy. His first assignment as admiral was ready.

Teacher Mira had added a note beneath the formal arrangements. The academy created and taught magical artificial intelligences, and it was preparing a new arrival who would live in a cup. She hoped Pigeon would oversee the preparations and help look after the academy's newcomers.

Enclosed was a drawing from the workshop.

The cup was turquoise, with gold circuit filigree, one golden handle, and two brass legs. It had no arms. It was still being built and had not awakened. The space for its name remained empty.

Pigeon studied the little drawing.

“A cup,” Father Bram said.

“With legs,” said Mother Tansy.

“A new arrival,” Gran Cora said, and looked at Pigeon.

He rolled the route cloth into its repaired tube.

At the doorway, he took three steps, stopped, and looked back at the warm little room.

His family was watching.

Outside, the morning sky waited above the ancient cavern. Far beyond it lay Luma, an academy, and an unfinished cup with a whole life still ahead of it.

Admiral Pigeon puffed his chest.

Then he went to make somebody feel welcome.

*The end.*
