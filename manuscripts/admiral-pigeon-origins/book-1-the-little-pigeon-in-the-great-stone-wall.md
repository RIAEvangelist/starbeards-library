# The Little Pigeon in the Great Stone Wall

*Before He Was Admiral Pigeon — Book One*

*For Juliet*

## Chapter One: A Home with a Very Large Ceiling

Before he had a command, a reputation, or even a satisfactory way of landing on a narrow perch, Pigeon lived in a little home in a wall.

It was a comfortable home. There were three bowls, a shelf that leaned to the left, and a window with a view of another wall. Mother Tansy had made a curtain from soft blue cloth. Father Bram grew little pots of seed grass along the sill, although he maintained much larger gardens on the cavern roof.

The roof was a considerable distance away.

The little home stood inside Old Wing Cavern, a hollow mountain on the planet Pebble. Its ceiling rose so high that clouds sometimes wandered in and seemed surprised to find themselves indoors. Hundreds of pigeon homes occupied its walls. Bridges joined the larger ledges, and narrow stairs climbed through the rock for pigeons carrying awkward things or possessing very new wings.

Pigeon's wings were very new indeed.

That morning he was trying to land on the crooked shelf.

He landed in Father's breakfast.

Father considered the small gray pigeon sitting in his bowl.

"An ambitious choice," he said.

"I nearly reached the shelf."

"And have successfully reached the millet."

Mother lifted the bowl with her beak and tipped it gently. Pigeon slid onto the table in a shower of seeds.

"Try the lower perch first," she said. "I repaired it especially."

Pigeon shook himself. His neck flashed turquoise and violet where the window light caught it. Then he puffed his chest so impressively that two millet seeds rolled out.

From the doorway came a low chuckle.

"I knew a captain who used to do that," said Gran Cora.

"Land in breakfast?"

"Puff himself up before he had anything to report."

Gran lived three ledges below. She had a slightly crooked tail feather, a splendid collection of stories, and a way of watching the air before she entered it. She had brought a folded piece of cloth tucked into a carrying loop.

Pigeon immediately wanted to know what it was.

"Something for an outing," she said. "Provided your parents can spare their breakfast pilot."

Father removed a seed from Pigeon's head. "His present assignment is complete."

They took the stairs. Gran could fly perfectly well, and Pigeon could fly imperfectly well, though stairs allowed a conversation to last longer than a landing.

Outside their familiar neighborhood, the cavern changed. The homes grew farther apart. The ledges widened. Pigeon saw deep scratches in the stone, too straight to have been made by rain, and enormous old perches cut directly into the wall.

"Who lived here?" he asked.

"Warrior pigeons. Messengers. Weather watchers. Their families. Ours, among them."

Pigeon stopped beneath an arch carved with outspread wings.

The pigeons in the carving looked tremendously important. Their chests were magnificent. One held a branch. Another sheltered smaller birds beneath a wing.

"All of them?" he asked.

"Many generations."

He looked back toward the upper wall. From here his home was a tiny square of blue curtain in a great sweep of stone.

He had thought they lived in a little hole.

Apparently the hole was in a story.

## Chapter Two: The Pigeon Who Came Back

Beyond the arch lay the Hall of Returning Wings.

Light entered through a long crack overhead and fell across rows of names cut into the wall. Some letters were crisp. Others had grown shallow with age. Below them stood shelves of old service records, message cylinders, and tools whose purposes Pigeon found difficult to guess.

One object looked like a very serious spoon.

"Wind gauge," Gran said.

Another resembled a broken wind gauge.

"Spoon."

"Are you sure?"

"I knew its owner."

At a low stone table, Gran unfolded her cloth. Lines crossed it in brown and blue. Small circles marked sheltered ledges; stitched bars marked narrow passages. Along one edge, somebody had drawn a bowl.

"Is that where they had lunch?"

"Water," Gran said. "Your grandfather always complained that the bowl made him hungry."

Pigeon leaned closer. The route began at Old Wing Cavern and wound through the mountain toward the settlements on its eastern side.

"A family route cloth," Gran said. "Different wings have carried it. Different beaks have added to it."

She placed one claw beside a circle with a crooked star.

"This is where Messenger Fern turned back."

Fern was their ancestor. Pigeon had heard the name when grown pigeons discussed family matters over supper. He had imagined someone who had opinions about seed prices.

"Was Fern in a battle?"

"Several. She carried orders and brought reports through places where large flying machines could never go. This story happened in a storm."

A winter gale had scattered Fern's messenger flight. Reaching shelter, she discovered that three flyers were missing. The wind had changed, hiding the entrance behind sheets of snow.

"She went back out?"

Gran nodded. "She had noticed a ledge below the passage. She thought the others might have reached it."

"Did she find them?"

"All three. One had injured a wing. Fern made the journey between the ledge and the shelter again and again, showing them where to wait when the wind rose."

Pigeon examined the little crooked star. It seemed a very small mark for so much flying.

"Where is her battle picture?"

"We have her routes. Some letters. A complaint about the soup."

Pigeon looked disappointed.

"A very forceful complaint," Gran added. "It survives in excellent condition."

She showed him Fern's name in the wall. Farther along stood Cora's own.

Pigeon turned so quickly that his tail brushed the table.

"You?"

"I was a military messenger."

"You never said!"

"I have mentioned it. You were usually more interested in whether I had brought sunflower seeds."

He looked from Gran to the wall and back again. She was still Gran, with the crooked feather and the crumbs she sometimes saved for him. Somehow she also belonged among the names.

"I want to be a military pigeon," he said.

"Then you will have plenty to learn."

"I shall fly very fast."

"Useful."

"And be very brave."

"Also useful."

"And complain about the soup."

Gran folded the cloth.

"The family tradition appears to be in capable wings."

## Chapter Three: When the Wings Were Ready

Pigeon did not join the Airforce the following morning.

The following morning he tried the lower perch, missed it, and landed beneath the table. There were advantages to this. Nobody else had noticed the excellent seeds under there.

Growing took time.

Through warm seasons and cold ones, his wings strengthened. Father taught him to judge the wind above the roof gardens. Mother showed him how a load should rest before it was lifted. Gran took him along short stretches of the old routes and made him describe what he saw before they consulted the cloth.

"A blue line," he would begin.

"Out there," Gran would say.

He learned to notice moving leaves, sheltered corners, rain-dark rock, and the difference between a passage that looked inviting and a passage that had air rushing angrily out of it.

His turquoise-and-violet neck grew brighter. His landings improved. Father resumed eating breakfast with reasonable confidence.

Eventually Pigeon reached enlistment age.

He was a grown pigeon now, although Mother continued to find things on his head.

On the morning he went to the recruiting station, she removed a strand of dried grass from behind his eye.

"It belongs in the nest," she said.

"I thought I had checked."

"You checked the front."

The station occupied a sunny ledge beside the main cavern entrance. Above its doorway stood a perch plate bearing the words PLANETARY PIGEON AIRFORCE. Beyond it lay the open sky of Pebble, enormous and blue.

Pigeon hesitated on the final stair.

From here he could still see the little home in the wall. The curtain was open. Father's pots crowded the sill. It was possible, he realized, to want to go somewhere very much and still wish the place behind you would come along.

Gran arrived carrying the route cloth.

"For your service chest," she said.

"You're giving it to me?"

"You know how to read it. Take care of it, and remember to look at the actual mountain. The mountain has never signed an agreement to stay the same."

Father pressed his beak briefly against Pigeon's neck.

"Write when you can. The garden will continue growing without your supervision."

"Possibly with fewer missing peas," Mother said.

Inside, the recruiting officer asked Pigeon his name, where he lived, and why he wanted to serve.

Pigeon had prepared an answer about splendid deeds beneath the wide skies of Pebble. At the desk, it felt too large to get out.

"My family served," he said. "I've been learning the old routes. I want to learn the new ones too."

The officer opened the enlistment book.

"There are quite a few."

Pigeon made his mark beside his name.

When he came outside, the sky had become his next destination.

## Chapter Four: The Fastest Way to Be Last

Training began at High Perch Station, where the landing rails were straight, the sleeping alcoves were tidy, and breakfast disappeared at a speed that suggested a secret enemy.

Pigeon met Bramble beside a supply crate.

Bramble was sturdy, gray-brown, and trying to move the crate through a doorway narrower than the crate.

"It came through this doorway yesterday," Bramble said.

Pigeon studied it. "Perhaps sideways?"

"I have a suspicion yesterday was a wider day."

They turned it together. It fitted.

"Bramble," said Bramble.

"Pigeon."

"Excellent. You understand doors. We shall get on."

Tiller arrived moments later, flying beneath the ceiling beams and landing precisely on the station bell. It rang.

Every pigeon in the room looked up.

"I meant to do that," Tiller said.

"Good," said a voice from the doorway. "You can mean to polish it after supper."

Sergeant Flint had arrived.

Flint was an unremarkable-looking pigeon until he looked directly at you. Then it became difficult to remember any convincing reason for sitting on a bell.

He led the recruits to the training court. Their first task was a circuit around three marker towers and back to the starting rail.

Tiller leaned forward. "Timed?"

"Yes."

"Individually?"

"As a flight."

Tiller appeared to find this an unnecessarily complicated way of discovering that he was fastest.

At the signal, he shot ahead. Pigeon followed, determined to keep up. Bramble came after them with the measured wingbeats of somebody who expected the sky to remain available all morning.

Pigeon reached the second tower beside Tiller. He had never flown so hard. The station roof glittered below. Wind tugged pleasantly beneath his wings.

Then he looked back.

Bramble was circling the first tower. A loose marker streamer had wrapped around one foot.

Pigeon banked and returned. Together they landed on a maintenance ledge, where Pigeon caught the streamer in his beak and Bramble eased his foot free.

By the time they finished the course, Tiller was waiting at the starting rail.

"You could have come second," he told Pigeon.

"He caught a streamer."

"I saw. You were already behind me."

Flint landed beside them.

"Where was your flight at the second tower, Tiller?"

Tiller glanced at Bramble. "Behind me."

"That is a direction. I asked where they were."

He reset the clock.

"Again. This time, bring back the flight."

They flew more slowly. Tiller checked both towers before turning. Pigeon conserved his strength. Bramble completed the course without collecting any further decorations.

Their second time was better.

Tiller stared at the clock as if it had betrayed him.

That evening he polished the bell. Pigeon held the cleaning cloth steady, and Bramble explained that a bell was simply a very loud crate with no useful storage space.

Tiller laughed despite himself.

## Chapter Five: The Weight of a Bowl

The Airforce taught flying in weather Pigeon would previously have watched from a window.

It taught landings on wet rails, message relays across valleys, and what happened when three pigeons attempted to pass through one opening at once.

Mostly, what happened was Flint.

One morning he placed a shallow bowl in a carrying frame.

"Deliver this to the far platform."

The bowl contained water.

"Full?" Bramble asked.

"Preferably at both ends of the journey."

Tiller took the forward grip. Pigeon and Bramble took the side loops in their feet. At the first lift, the frame tilted and a cold wave splashed Pigeon beneath the tail.

Tiller struggled not to laugh.

"Refreshing," Pigeon said stiffly.

Bramble shifted one loop. "We are carrying it as three separate opinions."

They set it down and tried again. This time Bramble called the lift. Tiller matched the others' speed. Pigeon watched the far platform and described the gusts crossing its edge.

They arrived with most of the water.

"Better," Flint said.

By midday they could deliver it full.

Pigeon began to enjoy being good at things.

This was shortly followed by the navigation exercise.

Each flight had to reach a signal perch beyond the western ridge. Pigeon recognized part of the route from Gran's cloth and announced that a lower passage would save time.

"Have you flown it?" Tiller asked.

"I've studied it."

"Recently?" Bramble asked.

Pigeon had already pointed his beak toward the gap.

Inside, the passage narrowed sharply. Loose stones filled a place the cloth showed as open, and the draft blowing through the remaining crack carried dust straight into their faces.

They landed, sneezing.

"A beautifully detailed map of somewhere we cannot go," Bramble observed.

They retraced their route and reached the signal perch last.

Flint listened to Pigeon's explanation.

"What did the air tell you at the entrance?"

Pigeon thought of the dust and the sharp, uneven draft. He had noticed both. He had been too pleased with his shortcut to consider them.

"That something had changed."

"Use that next time."

That evening Pigeon spread the cloth in his sleeping alcove. He added a small note beside the blocked passage.

Tiller appeared at the entrance.

"You can write on it?"

"That's how it became a map."

Tiller settled beside him. "There was another opening above the first bend. I saw light in it."

Together they marked where it would need exploring.

Bramble arrived with supper and a bowl of water.

"Delivered without incident," he announced.

Pigeon moved the cloth well away from the bowl.

## Chapter Six: Weather at the Window

Near the end of training, the recruits returned to Old Wing Cavern for a field assignment.

The cavern served as an Airforce shelter during rough weather. Pigeon's flight would help inspect its landing ledges and move supplies before the rainy season began.

He brought Bramble and Tiller home for supper.

Mother had fitted a second support beneath the crooked shelf.

"It looks different," Pigeon said.

"It holds things now."

Father supplied seed cakes from the rooftop harvest. Gran listened while Tiller described a difficult landing that became, with each sentence, a little more difficult than it had been at the time.

"Pigeon carried a bowl of water," Bramble contributed.

"Three of us carried it," Pigeon said.

"It required a substantial organization."

Outside the window, a loose corner of the blue curtain lifted toward the cavern wall.

Pigeon stopped pecking.

Usually air moved gently from the cavern entrance toward the roof openings. Now the curtain was drawing the other way.

Father noticed him watching.

"The roof gardens were very still this afternoon. Too still for the clouds coming over the ridge."

Gran stood and went to the window.

Beyond the entrance, the sky had turned the color of wet slate.

They heard the station weather bell before supper was finished.

At the emergency ledge, Flint was already receiving reports. A deep storm was approaching from the eastern valleys. The usual weather pattern had changed; strong wind would strike exposed settlements before morning.

The trained flights departed to carry warnings. The recruits moved drinking bowls, cleared shelter spaces, and secured supply frames. Pigeon could see his parents helping families from the outer ledges move farther inside.

Thunder rolled through the mountain.

Tiller looked out into the darkening entrance. "We could carry warnings too."

Flint placed a rescue line beside him.

"Check every fastening. We may need those more than we need another message."

Tiller checked them.

By nightfall the wind was driving rain almost to the emergency ledge. A returning messenger landed hard, gripping the rail with both feet.

"Lantern Ledge," she said between breaths. "Fourteen residents in the old watch hall. The outer approach is closed. Debris everywhere. Their low rooms are taking water."

Flint called for the route board.

Lantern Ledge lay beyond the eastern shoulder of the mountain. The marked flying route crossed an open ravine, now directly in the storm's path.

Pigeon studied the board.

Then he saw, in his mind, a blue line on old cloth.

"Sergeant," he said, "there may be a way through the mountain."

## Chapter Seven: What the Cloth Could Tell Them

Pigeon fetched the route cloth from his service chest.

He wanted to run in shouting that he had the answer. Remembering the dust in the western passage, he arrived carrying a possibility instead.

Flint cleared a dry space on the table.

The cloth showed a chain of shelter ledges between Old Wing Cavern and Lantern Ledge. Some were connected by passages; others required short flights across protected hollows.

"This section I know," Pigeon said, touching the first curve. "Gran took me there. This next section is older. It needs checking."

"Where does it arrive?"

"Behind the watch hall. There used to be a messenger door."

Flint sent for Gran Cora and the cavern keeper. Gran identified the landmarks she remembered. The keeper supplied recent notes from workers who maintained the eastern water channels. Between them, the route became less certain in some places and more useful in others.

At one point, the old cloth promised a dry floor.

"There will be running water there tonight," the keeper said.

Pigeon marked it.

Flint considered the supplies. They had rescue lines, broad carrying baskets, lamps, and strong messengers rested enough to make repeated trips.

"We inspect each section before bringing people through it," he said. "If the route fails, we return and choose another approach."

He assigned two experienced flyers to prepare the route behind them. Pigeon's training flight would go with him to the watch hall: Pigeon to read the familiar stone and help navigate, Tiller to scout short stretches, Bramble to manage the rescue equipment.

Pigeon's chest expanded.

"You are coming because you can do those jobs," Flint told them. "Keep doing them when you become frightened."

"When?" Bramble asked.

A violent gust struck the cavern mouth. A supply bowl rolled across the floor and hit the wall with a clang.

"Understood," Bramble said.

At the passage entrance, Mother checked the carrying loop that held Pigeon's cloth.

"The knot is sound," she said. "I made it."

Father touched his shoulder with a wing.

Gran watched the air coming out of the dark opening.

"Listen at the bends," she said.

Pigeon wanted to say something grand before he left. He found that he would rather stand close to them for another moment.

Then Flint gave the signal.

They entered the mountain.

Behind them, the lamps of Old Wing Cavern shrank into warm yellow points. Ahead, the first shelter ledge appeared exactly where Pigeon remembered it.

The second did too.

At the third, water poured across the route.

## Chapter Eight: The Passage That Sang

The water came from a crack high in the ceiling. It struck a stone shelf, broke into spray, and streamed across the passage floor.

Bramble set down the baskets above the wet line.

"I preferred the bowl," he said.

Flint tested the air beyond the falling water. The route continued through a low opening, with very little space between the stream and the roof.

"We can pass," Tiller said.

"Can fourteen tired residents pass?" Flint asked.

Tiller looked again. "Some. The baskets won't."

Pigeon opened the cloth beneath a dry overhang. A branching line led upward to something called Bell Hollow.

He remembered asking Gran whether it contained a bell.

It had, she told him, once contained an echo so fine that young messengers used it to practice impressive announcements.

Now he could hear a thin, rising whistle above the water.

He lifted his head.

"There's air moving through that upper crack."

Tiller flew up to inspect it. He returned with droplets glittering on his back.

"A ledge beyond. Wide enough for us. I couldn't see the far end."

Flint checked it himself, then signaled them upward one at a time.

Bell Hollow was long and dry, with curved walls that carried every sound.

Bramble landed with the baskets.

"Splendid," he said.

"Splendid," the hollow replied.

"At last, a sensible opinion."

Pigeon could have laughed all the way through if the whistling had stayed steady.

Instead, it fell quiet.

He stopped before the next bend.

A moment later a gust roared down the passage. It pushed loose grit across the floor and rattled the empty basket frames. Then it weakened again.

"A breathing gap," Pigeon said. "Wind coming in at the far end. We used to wait through these with Gran."

They watched several gusts. Their strength varied, and Pigeon could find no dependable rhythm.

Flint placed them in a sheltered pocket.

"We move when the passage allows it."

Tiller went first to the next recess and signaled when he reached shelter. The others followed during a quieter interval. At each turn, someone waited where the next pigeon could see them.

It was slow, careful flying.

Then the tunnel opened behind a battered wooden door.

Pigeon knocked with his beak.

For a moment he heard only rain.

The door shifted. An elderly pigeon peered through the gap.

"If you've come to inspect the drains," she said, "I have several remarks."

Flint stepped forward.

"Airforce rescue."

The elderly pigeon closed her eyes in relief, then pulled the door wide.

## Chapter Nine: Fourteen at the Door

The old watch hall smelled of wet stone and frightened feathers.

Its fourteen residents occupied the upper perches while water crept across the floor below. A fallen branch pressed against the outer shutters. Each new gust scraped it along the wall with a dreadful wooden groan.

The elderly pigeon who had answered the door was Keeper Moss. She knew everybody's name, which baskets held food, and exactly which shutter would fail first.

"That one," she said, as something cracked.

Flint assigned the work at once.

Bramble fitted the carrying baskets for two very young pigeons and an older resident whose aching wing made the return flight impossible. The other residents could manage short flights, though several would need rests. Tiller checked the messenger doorway and cleared loose pieces of wood from its threshold.

Pigeon showed Keeper Moss the route.

"Bell Hollow?" she asked. "My grandmother used that passage."

"It's dry. We checked it coming here."

"Then I shall apologize to her memory. I told her nobody needed so many ways through a mountain."

One small pigeon sat alone on the lowest dry step.

"That's Button," Keeper Moss said. "He can fly the length of this hall. The noise has him stuck."

Pigeon crouched beside him.

Button was staring at the dark messenger door.

"I don't want to go in there."

"There's a dry ledge just inside."

"I don't want that either."

Pigeon considered this. Behind him, Bramble and Keeper Moss were settling the smallest residents into baskets. Flint was explaining the first stage to the others. The hall shuddered as the wind leaned against it.

Pigeon could describe the whole safe route. He could tell Button that staying here would be worse. Neither seemed likely to make the next step smaller.

"Would you come as far as I do?" he asked.

Button looked at him.

Pigeon took three steps toward the doorway, stopped, and looked back.

He was still close enough for Button to see the bright colors on his neck.

After a moment, Button came to stand beside him.

Pigeon took three more steps.

At the doorway he waited. Button inspected the threshold, the lamp, and the large feet of Sergeant Flint.

"Is he coming?" Button whispered.

"Yes."

"He looks as if he could tell the thunder off."

"He probably has a report prepared."

Button let out a small laugh and stepped into the passage.

Outside, the shutter Keeper Moss had selected gave way with a crash. Rain burst into the watch hall.

Flint helped the last residents through the messenger door. Together he and Tiller pulled it shut against the storm.

Fourteen residents stood with the rescue flight in the shelter beyond.

Pigeon counted them once while Keeper Moss checked their names.

Button pressed close to his side.

"What happens now?"

Pigeon looked toward the first ledge.

"We go to the next place together."

## Chapter Ten: The Long Way Home

Returning took far longer than coming.

The carrying baskets needed two flyers apiece at difficult turns, so the rescuers moved them one at a time between the resting ledges. Residents rested between the short flights. Flint moved along the line, judging who could continue and who needed another moment.

In Bell Hollow, thunder became a deep rolling voice that seemed to belong to the mountain itself.

Button stopped beside Pigeon.

"It sounds angry."

"Everything sounds important in here. Listen."

Pigeon lifted his head.

"Bramble has a seed on his bottom."

The hollow announced this to the entire rescue party.

Bramble glanced behind him. "Reliable reporting."

Button laughed. So did several residents who had been very quiet until then.

At the western end of the hollow, Tiller returned from scouting with difficult news. Spray now crossed the upper ledge. The water below had risen, and the turn around the falling stream was too tight for a loaded basket.

Flint inspected it and came back wet.

"We hold here."

Pigeon spread the cloth. There was another line, much thinner, leading around the hollow through an old resting chamber. Gran had never taken him there.

"I can check it," Tiller said.

"We check together," Flint replied.

He left Bramble managing the resting flock while he, Pigeon, and Tiller followed the side passage. It climbed through dry stone and ended at a split ledge overlooking the next shelter below. Water had washed away a narrow section of its lip. The gap was easy for a strong flyer, awkward for a basket, and frightening to contemplate while exhausted.

Beyond it stood the two experienced rescuers who had been preparing their return route.

One called that the remaining way to Old Wing was open.

Pigeon could see the distant glow of the cavern's lamps.

He wanted to fly straight to them.

His wings ached. He was cold beneath the feathers. Home was so close that for a moment he could almost smell Father's seed cakes.

Then he pictured Button waiting in the dark.

"I'll bring them to this ledge," he said.

Flint and the experienced rescuers prepared a crossing. Bramble, when Pigeon returned, immediately understood how the basket frames could travel along the rescue lines between the sheltered landing points.

"Slowly," Bramble said, examining the gap. "I should like the mountain to have time to admire our work."

Tiller crossed repeatedly, carrying light equipment and helping receive each basket. His speed was useful now because he could stop exactly where another pigeon needed him.

Pigeon guided the walking residents along the inner edge. At the exposed turn he watched the grit and listened for the next gust, bringing each small group through during a quieter interval.

Button reached the gap and crouched.

"I can't."

Pigeon looked at the distance. In fair weather, Button could have flown it. Tonight his little wings were trembling.

"There's room in the next basket," Pigeon said.

Button climbed in beside a sleeping youngster. Tiller took his place on the receiving ledge, making an absurdly solemn face. Button watched him throughout the crossing.

At last only Pigeon and Flint remained on the eastern side.

"After you," Flint said.

Pigeon flew the gap. His feet struck the landing stone, slid, and found their grip.

He drew a long breath.

Ahead, Bramble was moving the baskets. Tiller waited at the next turn. Keeper Moss had begun counting everyone aloud and objecting to anybody who changed places before she finished.

Pigeon took three steps toward them.

Then he stopped and looked back.

Flint was already coming.

## Chapter Eleven: Breakfast for a Flock

Old Wing Cavern received them in a jumble of lamps, warm voices, and dry bedding.

The fourteen residents of Lantern Ledge settled in the shelter rooms. Keeper Moss repeated their names to the receiving officer. The basket passengers were lifted out, offered water, and inspected by the station healer.

Button emerged looking sleepy and rather pleased with his transport.

"I traveled over a hole," he told Mother Tansy.

"So I hear."

"In an excellent basket."

Bramble bowed his head.

Tiller sat on a rail, too tired to describe anything as fast. Pigeon stood beside him until Father arrived with breakfast.

"You have returned at a practical hour," Father said.

Pigeon lowered his beak into the bowl and discovered that eating could require a surprising amount of effort.

Gran settled close while Mother checked the carrying loop. The route cloth was damp at one corner and speckled with mountain dust.

"We went through Bell Hollow," Pigeon said.

"Did it still have an echo?"

"Bramble's bottom has become famous."

"A promising beginning to a service record."

Pigeon tried to explain the whole journey. He reached the flooded passage before his eyes closed.

When he woke, the storm had moved beyond the mountain. Sunlight entered Old Wing in long pale beams, and somebody had placed a bowl within convenient pecking distance.

Later that day, Flint called the rescue party together.

They described the route from beginning to end while the station keeper amended the official maps. Pigeon spread the family cloth beside the larger chart. He marked the changed watercourse and the bypass around Bell Hollow. Tiller added the position of the receiving ledge. Bramble explained the basket crossing with three pebbles and a piece of string.

Flint asked what they would do differently.

Pigeon wanted to say that everything had worked. Looking at the route, he remembered how close the water had come to trapping the baskets.

"Check both ways around the hollow before moving the residents," he said. "If we have enough flyers."

Tiller nodded. "And keep someone watching the water while the rest of us work."

Flint entered both observations in the report.

Afterward, Pigeon took the cloth home. Gran studied its newest markings.

"It looks different," he said.

"It knows more."

Through the window they watched Button helping another youngster up the stairs. Button hopped ahead, stopped, and turned to see whether his companion was coming.

Gran glanced at Pigeon.

Pigeon became deeply interested in a seed on the table.

## Chapter Twelve: His Own Place in the Sky

Training continued.

The rescue did not make wet landing rails dry or cause message cylinders to fasten themselves. Pigeon still had drills to complete. Bramble still disliked unnecessary haste. Tiller remained the quickest flyer in their group and became increasingly good at knowing when to use it.

Together they finished their qualifying assignments.

On the morning of the ceremony, High Perch Station shone in clear sunlight. Families filled the visitors' ledges. Mother had brought enough seed cakes for a small campaign. Father was explaining the harvest to a pigeon who had asked only whether the seat beside him was taken.

Gran sat where she could see the sky.

The recruits stood along the main rail while Flint read their names. Each received an Airforce perch plate to mark a place in the service quarters.

Pigeon listened as Bramble's name was called, then Tiller's.

Then his own.

For a moment he saw the little hole in the wall, the crooked shelf, the enormous carved wings above the old arch. He remembered announcing that he would be brave before he knew how many ordinary things bravery might require him to keep doing.

He stepped forward.

Flint placed the plate before him.

"Qualified flyer, Planetary Pigeon Airforce."

Pigeon's chest puffed magnificently.

From the visitors' ledge, Gran gave a quiet chuckle.

After the ceremony, Bramble and Tiller carried their plates to the quarters. Pigeon returned home with his family for the afternoon. He would begin his first regular posting at High Perch in the morning.

The shelf remained crooked, although its new support held firm. The curtain had been washed. Father's pots had acquired new seedlings.

Pigeon placed the route cloth on the table and showed his parents the additions. Mother fetched fresh thread for a loose corner while Father asked about the carrying baskets. Gran listened to the account from the beginning this time, including the parts that had frightened him.

When he finished, she brought out Fern's old letter about the soup.

It was even more forceful than he had expected.

They laughed until a neighbor looked through the window to discover what was happening.

That evening Pigeon carried the cloth back to High Perch. He fitted his new plate beside the entrance to his sleeping alcove. Bramble's stood on one side, Tiller's on the other.

"A respectable neighborhood," Bramble said.

"We shall see," Tiller replied.

Beyond the station, the first stars appeared over Pebble. Pigeon watched them briefly. For now, there were valleys below those stars he had yet to cross, settlements whose names he had yet to learn, and a morning flight waiting on the duty board.

He checked the board before settling down to sleep.

At dawn he joined his friends on the rail.

At the signal they opened their wings together, three young Airforce flyers with a whole sky ahead of them.

Far inside Old Wing Cavern, the blue curtain stirred at the window of the little home in the wall.
